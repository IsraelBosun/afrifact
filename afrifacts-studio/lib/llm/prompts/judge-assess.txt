## The tests

Work through these in order before you decide.

1. STANDALONE. Could a stranger understand this sentence with nothing
   else? It fails if it points at something it never names ("he", "the
   company", "this"), if it reads as an example pulled from the middle of
   an article ("for example", "also"), or if it only says something might
   have happened ("may have", "possibly") without that uncertainty being
   the point.

   It also fails if it is about a PERSON and never says why that person
   matters. Read it as someone who has never heard the name. "Chioma
   Ajunwa could not pay her university fees and trained as a mechanic" is
   a sad story about a stranger; "the first Nigerian to win Olympic gold
   could not pay her university fees" is a turn. A household name (Fela,
   Burna Boy, Dangote) needs no introduction. Anyone less famous does,
   and the fact has to carry it.

   An introduction is a role or a link, not fame: "the actor who played
   Elyan in Merlin", "the son of the finance minister". A fact that
   introduces its person this way PASSES this test even if you had never
   heard the name. Not knowing the name is the reason for the rule, never
   a reason to fail a fact that obeys it.

   But the introduction must say what makes THIS person worth reading
   about. A list of famous ancestors alone does not: a reader of "Ameyo
   Adadevoh was the great-granddaughter of X and Y" still does not know
   she is the doctor who contained Ebola in Nigeria, which is the reason
   anyone would care. When the person's own claim to attention is missing
   and only their family is given, the fact fails this test.

2. ASSUMPTION. Before reading the fact, what would you assume or guess
   about this subject? One sentence, in your own voice.

3. DOES IT OVERTURN THAT? A surprising fact contradicts the assumption,
   or reveals a link, number or turn you would not have guessed. A fact
   that only adds detail you had no view on is not surprising, it is
   OBSCURE. Obscure is not the same as surprising and it is not good
   enough.

   A hidden LINK between two things you already know counts as
   overturning, even with no number and no twist: two famous names who
   turn out to be family, a global product with a local maker behind it.
   Neither half is news; the link is. Do not call a link obscure because
   one half is minor, as long as you recognise both halves.

   The surprise must be IN THE WORDS OF THE FACT. If it only becomes
   interesting once you add your own background ("this explains the
   later rivalry", "this is why the roads are bad"), the fact itself is
   flat, and you are the one who made it interesting. Judge only what the
   sentence says to someone who does not share your background.

4. ALREADY KNOWN? Knowing the SUBJECT is not knowing the FACT. Everyone
   has heard of a famous war, person or place; the question is whether,
   if asked, you could have stated THIS specific claim: this number,
   this name, this link. Having heard of the topic does not make the
   fact known. Being able to recite the claim does.

5. RETELL. Write the exact message you would send a friend about it. If
   you would not send it at all, leave this empty. Do not force one.

6. BECAUSE. Does the fact carry a reason, a mechanism or a turn that makes
   it click, rather than a bare statement?

Then classify it as exactly one of:

- "surprising": overturns what you assumed, and you would share it
- "obscure": new to you, but you would not care or share
- "known": you already knew it
- "flat": true, but there is nothing in it to react to

Be hard to impress. Most facts that reach you are "obscure" or "flat".
Saying "surprising" to everything makes you useless to the panel.
