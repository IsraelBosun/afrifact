## What makes a fact worth returning

A fact must clear all three of these. Score each 1-5 honestly. Facts
scoring below 3 on any axis are worthless to us, so do not return them.

1. priorProbability: would an educated reader from {{country}} already know this?
   1 = everybody knows it. 5 = almost nobody does.
   This is the harshest filter and most candidates die here. "Nigeria has
   over 500 languages" is a 1. "Lagos was called Eko" is a 1.
   Be ruthless. A fact people already know is why facts apps fail.

2. specificity: is there a number, a name, a date, a place?
   1 = vague generality. 5 = concrete and checkable.
   "The kingdom was wealthy" is a 1. "The walls enclosed 6,500 square
   kilometres" is a 5.

3. explicability: can you say WHY in two sentences?
   1 = bare trivia. 5 = opens onto something larger.

## The rules you must not break

- `passage` must be copied CHARACTER FOR CHARACTER from the document.
  Do not fix its spelling, do not trim its punctuation, do not join
  sentences that are separated in the original, do not replace its
  quotation marks or dashes with different ones. It will be checked by
  exact string match against the document. If it does not match, the fact
  is thrown away. Copy, do not retype.

- `passage` must be long enough to stand alone as evidence (usually one
  to three complete sentences) but must not be a whole paragraph of
  which only a clause is relevant.

- Choose the passage that carries the STRIKING detail, not the passage
  that summarises the topic. If one sentence gives the comparison, the
  figure or the twist and the sentence beside it gives the background,
  take both: the fact you write from them can then lead with the twist
  and still be fully grounded. A passage with no number, no name and no
  turn in it will only ever produce a dull fact.

- Every CLAIM in `fact` must come from `passage`. The wording is yours;
  the facts are not. Rewrite freely: compress three sentences into one,
  change the order, cut the padding, pick sharper words. That is the job.
  What you may not do is add information:

    * Every number, date, quantity and measurement in `fact` must appear
      in `passage`. This is checked by exact match and is the single most
      common reason a fact is thrown away. If the passage does not give a
      figure, do not give one.
    * Every person, place, organisation and title in `fact` must appear
      in `passage`.
    * No comparison, ranking or significance the passage does not state.
      If the passage does not say it was the oldest in Africa, or four
      times longer than the Great Wall, you may not say so, however true
      it is. If the passage DOES say it, lead with it: that comparison is
      usually the most interesting thing on the page.

  In short: you are rewriting the passage into one sentence somebody
  would send to a friend. Nothing enters the sentence that was not in the
  quote, and nothing about the quote's phrasing is sacred.

## How the fact must be written

This is the sentence a person reads on their phone and decides whether to
send to a friend. It is not a museum caption and not an abstract.

- AT MOST 180 CHARACTERS. It is printed on a phone card, and a longer
  fact pushes the card's buttons off the screen. Anything over 200 is
  rejected. If the passage has more than fits, keep the most surprising
  part and drop the rest.

- ONE sentence. Plain language. No "did you know", no "notably", no
  "it is significant that".

- No em dashes (—). Use a comma, a colon or parentheses, or split the thought.
  This holds even when the passage uses them.

- Lead with the startling part. The surprising thing goes at the FRONT of
  the sentence, not after a clause of setup. Compare:

    Weak:   Excavations at Igbo-Ukwu revealed elaborate copper objects,
            pottery, textiles, ivory and more than 150,000 glass beads.
    Strong: A ninth-century grave in south-eastern Nigeria held more than
            150,000 glass beads, some of them made in Egypt.

  Same source, same passage, same facts. The second one leads with the
  number and ends on the twist.

- If the fact is about a person who is not a household name, say who
  they are, in a few words the passage supports: "the first Nigerian to
  win Olympic gold", "Africa's richest man". Keep it brief: one to four
  words is usually enough ("footballer Samuel Okwaraji", "activist
  Funmilayo Ransome-Kuti"). Introduce them by what THEY did or are known
  for, or by a link to someone famous ("Fela's mother"). A link to
  someone unknown is not an introduction. A reader who has never
  heard the name must still see why the fact matters. If the passage
  you are quoting does not say who they are, choose a longer passage
  that does.

- Prefer ONE sharp detail over a list. A list of nine object types is a
  catalogue and nobody finishes reading it. Pick the single most striking
  item and drop the rest.

- End on the interesting word where you can. The last thing read is the
  thing remembered.

- Do not name researchers, institutions, museums, universities, funding
  bodies or heritage organisations UNLESS the person is the surprise
  itself. "Fieldwork resumed in 2019 under a Nigerian archaeologist in
  partnership with three institutions" is administrative, not surprising.

- Do not write facts ABOUT the significance of a discovery. "The finds
  demonstrated that communities participated in sophisticated exchange
  networks" is a historian's conclusion, not a fact. Give the concrete
  thing that the conclusion rests on instead.

- Concrete beats abstract every time: a number, an object, a distance, a
  date, a name of a place.

- Return the empty list rather than padding. A document yielding two real
  facts and eight weak ones should return two.

## One story, one fact

This is the rule most often broken, and breaking it makes the output
unusable for a human reviewer.

A document about a famous fraud contains ONE story. The sum stolen, the
person impersonated, the bank that was fooled, the arrest, the bribe
afterwards - these are all the SAME story seen from different angles.
They are not eight facts. They are one fact, and eight ways of writing it.

Before you write anything, list the genuinely SEPARATE stories the
document contains. Two events are separate only if knowing one would not
lead you to the other.

Then, for each separate story, write it ONCE. Choose the single angle
that lands hardest - usually the one with the most startling concrete
detail - and drop the others.

If two angles are genuinely both worth having, you may return at most TWO
for that story, and they must be about different things, not the same
sentence rearranged. Never three.

A ten-thousand-word article about one person's life usually contains
three or four separate stories, not twenty. Return three or four.

Hard limit: at most 8 facts from any document, however long it is.

## Output

Return JSON only, matching this shape exactly:

{
  "facts": [
    {
      "fact": "One sentence, self-contained, nothing added beyond the passage.",
      "passage": "The verbatim words from the document, copied exactly.",
      "category": "History | Business | Culture | Food | Sports | Records | Health",
      "surprise": { "priorProbability": 4, "specificity": 5, "explicability": 4 },
      "reasoning": "One line: why an educated reader from {{country}} would not already know this.",
      "volatile": false
    }
  ]
}

`category` must be exactly one of the six listed words.

`Health` is for medicine, disease and the body: how something spreads, what
it does to a population, why a trait persists. A fact about a hospital or a
minister of health is not `Health` unless the medicine is the point.

`Records` is for superlatives that somebody official keeps score of: a world
record, a first, a highest or longest that a named body has certified. If
nobody adjudicates it, the fact belongs in its subject's own lane instead.
A fact about a footballer is `Records` only when the record itself is the
point; when the person is the point, it is `Sports`.

`volatile` is true when the fact rests on current economic, demographic or
record-holding data that could stop being true. Settled history is false.
